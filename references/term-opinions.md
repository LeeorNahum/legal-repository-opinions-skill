# Term Opinions

Default positions for an agreement among the owners of a venture. Each is a default with its reason. Depart from one when the deal calls for it. Where a point varies, the line says what it varies by, such as the state or how the entity is taxed. Find that out for the matter before relying on it.

## Roles And Control

- Describe each owner's role by a lasting function, such as pitching, not by an artifact of the moment, such as one pitch deck. The artifact gets finished or dropped, and the clause then describes nothing.
- Say once who has the final say in what, and which decisions need every owner. A rule stated in two places ends up stated two ways.
- Where ownership is equal, state the equal partnership in a sentence: doing more work or putting in more money does not change the split or the say unless the agreement says so. This is the argument equal owners have later, and silence leaves it open.

## Vesting

- Vesting dates fall on the 1st of the month. Every month has a 1st, so no date needs a rule for a short month.
- The schedule states its first and its last vesting date. Two stated dates settle whether the count starts at the start date or a period after it.
- One worked example shows what a leaver keeps. A reader who misreads the formula finds out before signing.
- Before signing an agreement that creates vesting, check whether a tax election with a short deadline applies, and record the deadline. In the United States it is the election under section 83(b) of the Internal Revenue Code: a person who receives property for their work while it can still be forfeited, such as unvested ownership, may choose to be taxed on its value when received, not as it vests. The election must be made no later than 30 days after the transfer. Work out from the facts whether the agreement makes such a transfer and on what date. A missed deadline is the costly mistake here, and it passes before anyone thinks about tax.

## Leaving

- A leaver, or the estate of an owner who dies, keeps the vested share as an economic right: its percentage of what the venture pays its owners, with no vote. The share was earned, and the people still working have to be able to run the venture.
- No forced buyout by default. A buyout needs a price rule, the price rule is the hard part, and a bad one is worse than none. Add one only when the owners have agreed how the price is set.
- A transfer of a share needs the other owners' written agreement. Owners chose each other, not whoever a share is later sold to.

## Money

- Keep four money terms distinct and use each word for one thing: sales, what comes in. Profit, what is left after costs. A payout to owners, what the venture actually pays them. Pay for work, a salary or fee. "Half the money" can mean any of the four.
- Say who decides a payout to owners. The default is every owner together, so that no one can drain the venture or starve the others.
- A company taxed as a partnership pays out at least enough to cover each owner's tax on its profit, when it has the money. In the United States, a company with two or more owners that is taxed as a partnership pays no federal income tax itself. Its owners pay tax on their shares of the profit whether or not it is paid out, so without this floor an owner can owe tax on money never received. This turns on how the company is taxed: a limited liability company with two or more owners is taxed as a partnership unless it chose to be taxed as a corporation, so find out which applies.

## Background Technology

- What a party owned before, or keeps as its own project apart from the product's code, stays that party's. Otherwise working on the venture quietly costs someone their other work.
- If the party puts any of it into the product, the venture gets a free, permanent right to keep using the version it has. The product must not break when that party leaves.
- That right does not cover a service the party runs. The venture uses a service on the terms offered to anyone, because a right to use code is not a promise to operate something for free forever.

## Notices And Names

- Notices go to the address a party gives at signing, usually the email address in the signature block, or a newer one given in writing. An address typed into the body goes stale and takes an amendment to change.
- Where the agreement accepts a text message or an email as writing, say so once, in one section. Otherwise every "in writing" reopens the question.
- A company name in the agreement carries the designator its state requires, such as LLC. The required words vary by state, so look up the forming state's rule and the name as registered. A name written without the designator may not match the registered one.
